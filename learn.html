Money In And Money Out

The profit and loss statement (P&L) shows what the business earned and spent over a year. These are its main lines, from top to bottom.

Revenue (Sales)

All the money the business made from customers before taking anything off. Also called sales, turnover or income.

Bakery exampleThe bakery sold $500,000 of bread, cakes and coffee this year.

Revenue is the top line. Everything else on the P&L is taken away from it.

Cost Of Goods Sold

What it cost to make or buy the things the business sold, like ingredients or stock. Also called cost of sales.

Bakery exampleFlour, butter, coffee beans and packaging cost the bakery $200,000.

This cost goes up and down with sales: sell more cakes, buy more flour.

Gross Profit

What's left from sales after paying for the goods themselves.

FormulaRevenue − Cost of goods sold

Bakery example$500,000 − $200,000 = $300,000

Gross profit has to pay for everything else: rent, wages, power and more.

Operating Expenses

The everyday costs of running the business that aren't the goods themselves, like rent, wages, power, insurance and marketing.

Bakery exampleRent, staff and other running costs came to $230,000.

These costs mostly stay the same even when sales change, so a quiet month hurts.

Operating Profit

Profit from the business's main work, before interest on loans and tax.

FormulaGross profit − Operating expenses

Bakery example$300,000 − $230,000 = $70,000

Also called EBIT (earnings before interest and tax).

Net Income (Profit)

The bottom line: what's left after every cost, interest and tax. If it's negative, the business made a loss.

FormulaOperating profit − Interest − Tax

Bakery example$70,000 − $10,000 interest − $15,000 tax = $45,000

A business can make a profit and still run short of cash. See Free Cash Flow below.

Margins

A margin turns a profit into a percentage of sales, so you can compare a small business with a big one, or this year with last year.

Gross Margin

How many cents of each sales dollar are left after paying for the goods.

FormulaGross profit ÷ Revenue

Bakery example$300,000 ÷ $500,000 = 60%

Normal gross margins are very different between industries: a software company might be near 80%, a supermarket nearer 25%.

Operating Margin

How many cents of each sales dollar are left after running costs too.

FormulaOperating profit ÷ Revenue

Bakery example$70,000 ÷ $500,000 = 14%

If gross margin is healthy but operating margin is thin, running costs are probably the problem.

Net Margin

How many cents of each sales dollar end up as profit.

FormulaNet income ÷ Revenue

Bakery example$45,000 ÷ $500,000 = 9%

In the Flash Analyst Health Score, a net margin of 20% or more gets full marks for profitability.

Growth

Is the business getting bigger or smaller? Growth compares sales over time.

Revenue Growth

How much sales went up or down compared with last year.

Formula(This year − Last year) ÷ Last year

Bakery exampleLast year the bakery sold $450,000. ($500,000 − $450,000) ÷ $450,000 = 11.1%

A negative number means sales fell.

CAGR

Compound annual growth rate: the steady yearly growth that would take sales from where they started to where they are now. It smooths out up-and-down years.

Formula(Last ÷ First)1 ÷ years − 1

Bakery exampleThree years ago the bakery sold $400,000. (500,000 ÷ 400,000)1/3 − 1 = 7.7% a year

In the Health Score, sales growth of 15% a year or more gets full marks for growth.

What It Owns And Owes

The balance sheet is a snapshot of one day, usually the last day of the year. It always balances: what the business owns = what it owes + what belongs to the owners.

Assets

Everything the business owns that has value: cash, money customers owe it, stock, equipment, vehicles and property.

Bakery exampleThe bakery's ovens, van, stock and cash add up to $400,000.

Current assets are the ones that will turn into cash within a year, like cash, stock and money owed by customers. The bakery has $120,000 of them.

Liabilities

Everything the business owes: bills to suppliers, loans, tax and wages due.

Bakery exampleThe bakery owes $150,000 in total.

Current liabilities are the ones due within a year. The bakery has $80,000 of them.

Equity

The part of the business that belongs to its owners on paper: what it owns minus what it owes.

FormulaAssets − Liabilities

Bakery example$400,000 − $150,000 = $250,000

If liabilities are bigger than assets, equity is negative. That's a serious warning sign.

Key Ratios

Ratios compare two numbers to answer one question about the business. Flash Analyst works out 12 of them as live Excel formulas.

Current Ratio

Can the business pay the bills due this year from things it can turn into cash this year?

FormulaCurrent assets ÷ Current liabilities

Bakery example$120,000 ÷ $80,000 = 1.5x

Below 1x means bills due soon are bigger than short-term assets. In the Health Score, 2.0x or more gets full marks.

Debt To Equity

How much the business relies on money it owes compared with the owners' own money.

FormulaLiabilities ÷ Equity

Bakery example$150,000 ÷ $250,000 = 0.6x

Lower usually means safer. In the Health Score, 0.5x or less gets full marks, and 3.0x or more gets none.

Return On Assets (ROA)

How much profit the business makes from everything it owns.

FormulaNet income ÷ Assets

Bakery example$45,000 ÷ $400,000 = 11.3%

Useful for comparing businesses that need lots of equipment with ones that don't.

Return On Equity (ROE)

How much profit the business makes for each dollar the owners have in it.

FormulaNet income ÷ Equity

Bakery example$45,000 ÷ $250,000 = 18%

A very high ROE can come from lots of debt rather than great results, so check debt to equity too.

Interest Coverage

How many times operating profit could pay the interest on the business's loans.

FormulaOperating profit ÷ Interest

Bakery example$70,000 ÷ $10,000 = 7.0x

Below 1x means profit doesn't even cover the interest.

Cash

Profit isn't the same as cash. Customers might pay late, and buying equipment uses cash without counting as a cost straight away.

Operating Cash Flow

The cash that actually came in from running the business during the year.

Bakery exampleThe bakery's cash from running the business was $65,000.

Found on the cash flow statement as "net cash from operating activities".

Capital Expenditure (Capex)

Cash spent on long-lasting things like equipment, vehicles or buildings.

Bakery exampleThe bakery bought a new oven for $20,000.

Capex keeps the business running and growing, but it uses cash.

Free Cash Flow

The cash left over after running the business and paying for equipment. It can repay loans, pay the owners or be saved.

FormulaOperating cash flow − Capex

Bakery example$65,000 − $20,000 = $45,000

Negative free cash flow for a year or two can be fine when a business is investing, but not forever.

Free Cash Flow Margin

How many cents of each sales dollar turn into free cash.

FormulaFree cash flow ÷ Revenue

Bakery example$45,000 ÷ $500,000 = 9%

In the Health Score, 15% or more gets full marks for cash generation.
